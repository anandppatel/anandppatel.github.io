The observation

Three altitudes, one point.

For every noncollinear triangle, the three altitude lines meet at a unique point H, called the orthocenter.

Why H exists

Draw a parallel through each vertex to make a larger triangle; its perpendicular bisectors turn out to be our original altitudes.

This proof uses the result from the circumcenter episode: the perpendicular bisectors of a noncollinear triangle meet at a unique point.

Why A lies between Q and R

In parallelogram ABCQ, AQ points in the direction from B to C. In parallelogram ACBR, AR points from C to B. These directions are opposite, and both lengths equal BC > 0, so Q, A, and R occur in that order.

Why we use altitude lines

For an obtuse triangle, some altitudes meet the extensions of the opposite sides, and H lies outside the triangle. The complete lines always meet, even when the altitude segments do not.

For a right triangle, H is the right-angle vertex: the two legs already lie on altitude lines. The line through each vertex perpendicular to its opposite side remains well defined even when H is that vertex.

Another way to see the center

The orthocenter as a weighted-string equilibrium

For an acute triangle, put a frictionless pulley at each vertex. Tie three light, inextensible strings to a freely moving ring and pass one string over each pulley. Hang counterweights with masses proportional to the opposite side lengths: a = BC at A, b = CA at B, and c = AB at C. The ring’s unique equilibrium is the orthocenter H.

The acute hypothesis matters. For a right or obtuse triangle, this same apparatus minimizes its energy at the right or obtuse vertex. In the right case that vertex is H; in the obtuse case H is outside the triangle and is not the string equilibrium. A real apparatus needs a stop at the pulley to realize the limiting vertex position.

Explore the weighted strings

Move the triangle through acute, right, and obtuse shapes. Compare H with the energy-minimizing position of the ring. The model uses constant ideal tensions proportional to the opposite sides; hanging counterweights supply those tensions at rest. The conclusion changes when an angle reaches 90°.

Why H minimizes the weighted distances only in the stated cases

As the ring moves, each hanging weight rises by the change in its string’s horizontal length. Apart from a positive common factor and a constant, the gravitational potential energy is

F(P) = a · PA + b · PB + c · PC.

This function is strictly convex: equality in the convexity inequality for a distance to a fixed vertex would force that vertex and both proposed ring positions onto one line; equality for all three terms would force A, B, and C to be collinear. Thus an equilibrium, if found, is the unique global minimum.

When ABC is acute, H lies inside. Rotate the three directed side vectors BC, CA, AB by the same 90° toward the interior. The rotated vectors point along HA, HB, HC and have lengths a, b, c. Since the original three vectors add to zero, their rotations add to zero too. These are exactly the three tension vectors at H. Their sum vanishes, proving the minimum there.

Suppose instead that A ≥ 90°. At A, the combined pull of the B- and C-strings has squared magnitude

b2 + c2 + 2bc cos A ≤ b2 + c2 − 2bc cos A = a2.

It can therefore be balanced by a tension of magnitude at most a at A. More formally, let w be that combined pull and let u = P − A. The supporting-line inequality for each distance gives F(P) − F(A) ≥ a|u| − w · u ≥ 0. Strict convexity gives the unique minimum at A. The cases at B and C are identical.

The weighted-distance characterization for acute triangles appears in Gueron and Tessler, Proposition 1, p. 450. The force balance and convexity argument here establish the precise domain used by the experiment.

One rotating experiment joins G, N, and H

Fix three identical springs at A, B, and C on a horizontal table, and join their other ends to one bead. Each spring has stiffness k and zero natural length. Rotate the table about the circumcenter O at angular speed ω. View the bead from the rotating table. As the speed increases, its equilibrium travels along the Euler line: from the centroid G, through the nine-point center N, to the orthocenter H.

Let m be the bead’s mass and put λ = mω2/k. The three settings are λ = 0 at G, λ = 1 at N, and λ = 2 at H. The table is stationary at the first setting. If O = H, all these positions coincide.

Explore the rotating springs

Change the speed parameter and move the triangle. The drawing uses the rotating view, in which the three anchors stay fixed. Follow the equilibrium point and the balance of spring and centrifugal forces.

Why the equilibrium follows the Euler line

In the following equations, letters denote position vectors. The three spring forces add to 3k(G − P), while the centrifugal force is mω2(P − O). A bead at rest relative to the table therefore satisfies

3(G − P) + λ(P − O) = 0.

The Euler-line identity 3G = 2O + H gives

P = O + (H − O)/(3 − λ).

This proves all three stated settings. It also proves stability throughout the displayed interval 0 ≤ λ ≤ 2. Indeed, the effective potential in the rotating frame is

U(P) = ½k(PA2 + PB2 + PC2) − ½mω2PO2.

If Pλ denotes the equilibrium, completing the square gives U(P) − U(Pλ) = ½k(3 − λ)|P − Pλ|2. This is positive away from Pλ. Coriolis force does no work; gentle damping relative to the table makes a displaced bead settle. Without damping, stability does not mean that motion stops.

This apparatus is a direct consequence of the Euler-line identity and the stated force laws. The calculation establishes the interpretation; the animation illustrates it.
