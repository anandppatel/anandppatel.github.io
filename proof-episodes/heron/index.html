The theorem

Heron’s formula

For a noncollinear triangle with side lengths a, b, c and semiperimeter s = (a + b + c)/2, its area is √(s(s − a)(s − b)(s − c)).

Before the proof

The area equals sr; can the incircle and an excircle express r using only the side lengths?

The book’s exercise proof

This episode follows Gerard A. Venema, Exploring Advanced Euclidean Geometry with GeoGebra (2013), §4.5, Figure 4.4 on printed p. 44 and Exercises 4.5.1–4.5.7 on printed p. 45 (PDF pages 61–62). Exercise 4.5.2 supplies the tangent-length bookkeeping; 4.5.4 and 4.5.5–4.5.6 supply the two similarities; 4.5.7 states the final formula.

Which points are where?

E is the A-excenter, rather than a side midpoint. The incircle touches BC, CA, AB at X, Y, Z. The A-excircle touches BC at an interior point T, and the extensions of AC and AB beyond C and B at U and V. The Nagel episode establishes the excircle construction and these positions. These statements hold for acute, right, and obtuse triangles.

Why neither similarity collapses

The tangent lengths x = s − b, y = s − c, z = s − a and both radii are strictly positive for a noncollinear triangle. Thus AZI, AVE, ZBI, and VEB are genuine right triangles even when ABC is right or obtuse. X and T can coincide in an isosceles triangle; no step requires them to be distinct.

Keep the two angle correspondences separate

The first similarity is AZI ∼ AVE, with A ↔ A, Z ↔ V, I ↔ E. The second is ZBI ∼ VEB, with Z ↔ V, B ↔ E, I ↔ B. At B, the internal and external half-angles are complementary, not equal.

An exact formula and a numerical picture

The proof establishes an exact area identity. Decimal measurements and labels joined by ≈ only report the drawing’s precision; they do not establish exact equality or point coincidence. Very flat triangles can have an A-excircle far beyond the triangle; use Fit construction to see it.
