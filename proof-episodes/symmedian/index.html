The theorem

The symmedian point K

Reflect each median ray across its vertex’s internal angle bisector; the three resulting symmedians meet at a unique interior point K, called the symmedian point.

Why the symmedian matters

Before the proof

The medians are concurrent: will reflecting each in a different line preserve that concurrence?

What we use

  • C2: rigid motions and reflection

A ray is reflected

Reflect the ray from a vertex through the side midpoint, then intersect the reflected ray with the opposite side; the reflected midpoint itself need not lie on that side.

Why the reflected ray meets the side

A median ray is strictly inside its vertex angle; reflection in the internal bisector exchanges the two boundary rays and preserves the interior, so the reflected ray meets the opposite segment exactly once.

The sine ratio reverses

Reflection exchanges the two parts of the vertex angle, so the numerator and denominator of its sine ratio exchange places.

Equal-sided triangles are included

If the two sides at a vertex are equal, its median is already the internal bisector and reflection fixes the ray; the proof still applies, including to an equilateral triangle.

The drawing is a construction

Each ray is reflected independently; the proof derives their concurrence, while numerical calculations only position the picture.

Connections to explore

Why the symmedian matters

The symmedian links three ways of describing a triangle: angles, lengths, and areas. Its concurrence is the first part of a larger pattern.

The isogonal conjugate of the centroid

Start with any interior point P and reflect AP, BP, and CP in the three internal angle bisectors. Trigonometric Ceva shows that the reflected rays meet again. Their common point is called the isogonal conjugate of P; reflecting twice returns P. The centroid G lies on the medians, so its isogonal conjugate is K. The incenter I stays fixed because its three rays are already angle bisectors. These are three different reflections, not one reflection of the whole triangle.

A squared ratio identifies the symmedian

For a cevian AD with D inside BC, compare the three rules below. The squared ratio gives a practical way to recognize a symmedian from lengths, even when its defining reflection is not visible.

Angles lead to areas and distances

Write a = BC, b = CA, c = AB. The triangles KBC, KCA, KAB have areas in the ratio a² : b² : c². Consequently, the perpendicular distances from K to the lines BC, CA, AB have ratio a : b : c. The incenter has equal distances to the sides; K has distances proportional to their lengths. The homework uses these tools in new configurations: Problem 3 locates K in a right triangle, and Problem 4 constructs a symmedian with tangents to the circumcircle.

A point selected by a minimum

There is also an optimization interpretation: among points strictly inside the triangle, K uniquely minimizes the sum of the squares of the three perpendicular distances to the full side lines. This claim concerns squared distances, not their ordinary sum. To investigate it, first add the areas of PBC, PCA, and PAB for an arbitrary interior point P; then compare its distances with the proportional distances at K. The area identity and a sum of nonnegative squares supply the proof.
