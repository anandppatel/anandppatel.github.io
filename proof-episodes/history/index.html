Historical notes

Early surviving accounts of the triangle centers and the results in our proof episodes.

A theorem’s name need not identify its first discoverer. These notes distinguish an early text we can locate from a later attribution. They do not claim to settle priority in every case.

Ancient dates refer approximately to the works, not to the surviving manuscripts or modern translations. Each source gives a book, proposition, section, or printed page to help you find the result. The episodes use modern notation and may give a different proof.

Archimedes · Third century BCE

The centroid G: a center of gravity

Archimedes studied the point we call the centroid as the center of gravity of a uniform triangular plate. In Book I, Proposition 13 of On the Equilibrium of Planes, he places this center on a line joining a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side. Proposition 14 identifies it as the intersection of two such lines.

The surviving treatise gives an early account of the centroid through the geometry of balance. Our episode establishes concurrence through areas. These propositions document the result; they do not establish when someone first discovered it. Proposition 14 identifies the intersection, rather than stating the familiar 2:1 ratio as its conclusion.

Euclid · Around 300 BCE

The circumcenter O: Euclid’s circle through three vertices

Book IV, Proposition 5 of Euclid’s Elements constructs a circle through the three vertices of a triangle. Euclid bisects two sides and draws perpendiculars through their midpoints. He shows that their intersection is equally distant from the three vertices, so it serves as the center of the required circle.

Euclid considers the intersection inside the triangle, on a side, and outside it. Those cases will be familiar from moving the vertices in our episode. This is a precise ancient account of the circumcenter construction, although Euclid does not use our label O or identify its first discoverer.

Al-Nasawī · Early eleventh century

The orthocenter H: an explicit medieval proof

An explicit proof of altitude concurrence survives in al-Nasawī’s commentary in the Book of Lemmas tradition. Al-Nasawī, active around 1010 CE, starts with the intersection of two altitudes and proves that the line through this point and the third vertex is perpendicular to the opposite side. Jan Hogendijk’s edition and English translation let us read this argument directly.

The translated passage identifies this auxiliary lemma as al-Nasawī’s. It is distinct from al-Kūhī’s circle theorems discussed in the same article. The Book of Lemmas traditionally bears Archimedes’ name, but that tradition does not establish that Archimedes wrote the surviving compilation. This is a documented early proof, with no claim here that al-Nasawī was the first discoverer.

Euclid · Around 300 BCE

The incenter I: Euclid’s inscribed circle

Immediately before his circumcircle construction, Euclid constructs the incircle in Elements IV.4. He intersects two internal angle bisectors, drops perpendiculars to the three sides, and uses congruent right triangles to show that those perpendicular distances are equal. A circle with that common radius touches all three sides.

Euclid presents a circle construction. The modern statement that the three internal angle bisectors meet at the incenter expresses the same geometry using the angle-bisector characterization. The proposition is an early surviving source for the construction; it makes no claim to identify its original discoverer.

Euler · Written 1763; published 1767

The Euler line: three centers in one relation

Euler’s Solutio facilis problematum quorundam geometricorum difficillimorum studies the relations among triangle centers. For a non-equilateral triangle, it establishes that the circumcenter, centroid, and orthocenter lie on one line, with the centroid dividing the circumcenter–orthocenter segment in the ratio 1:2. In our notation, GH = 2GO.

The Euler Archive dates the paper’s composition to 1763 and its publication to 1767. The date 1765 often attached to this result is the year assigned to the journal volume. Euler’s labels also differ from ours: his E, F, H correspond to our H, G, O. Our episode’s constructed point X and similar triangles are a modern presentation of the relation, not a transcription of his argument.

Read the sources

  • Original work and publication record Euler Archive: E325Novi Commentarii academiae scientiarum Petropolitanae 11, pp. 103–123, especially p. 114. The archive records the separate writing and publication dates.
  • Historical reading guide Mackay, History of the Nine-point CirclePrinted p. 19 distinguishes Euler’s triangle-center paper from the later nine-point-circle article and points to Euler’s original p. 114.

Euclid · Around 300 BCE

The angle-bisector theorem, including its converse

The angle-bisector theorem appears explicitly in Elements VI.3. Euclid states both directions: an internal angle bisector divides the opposite side in the ratio of the adjacent sides, and that ratio conversely identifies the bisector. His argument introduces a parallel line and compares the resulting segments.

This gives us a precise ancient text to read alongside the episode’s area argument. The theorem and its converse were already presented together in the Elements. As with the circle constructions, the surviving account does not establish that Euclid was the first person to discover the result.

Aristotle · Fourth century BCE; Euclid · Around 300 BCE

The right angle in a semicircle: a theorem and a later attribution

The right angle in a semicircle was already a familiar example in Aristotle’s fourth-century BCE writings. Posterior Analytics II.11 discusses why this angle is right. Euclid later gives a systematic geometric proof in Elements III.31.

The name Thales’ theorem rests on a different kind of evidence. Writing many centuries after Thales, Diogenes Laertius reports Pamphila’s claim that Thales first inscribed a right triangle in a circle; he also records a competing attribution to Pythagoras. That passage supplies neither a proof by Thales nor an explicit statement about a diameter. It preserves a later attribution, rather than securely identifying the theorem’s first discovery.

Read the sources

  • Early surviving account in translation Aristotle, Posterior Analytics II.11Part 11 on this page; standard text location 94a27–34. English translation by G. R. G. Mure.
  • Ancient geometric proof in translation Euclid, Elements III.31The first assertion concerns the right angle in a semicircle.
  • Later testimony about attribution Diogenes Laertius, Lives of Eminent Philosophers I.24–25R. D. Hicks translation, vol. I, pp. 25–27; the report attributed to Pamphila and the competing attribution.

Brianchon and Poncelet · January 1821

The nine-point circle: all nine points in an 1821 paper

Brianchon and Poncelet’s Théorème IX gives an explicit early statement and proof of the full nine-point theorem. It includes the three altitude feet, three side midpoints, and three midpoints between the orthocenter and vertices. Their article appeared in the issue dated 1 January 1821. All nine points are already present together in that publication.

The theorem there concerns the nine points lying on one circle; it does not state the center and radius in the form used in our episodes. Feuerbach’s 1822 book develops further properties of the circle, including its center midway between the circumcenter and orthocenter. Earlier related investigations make a single first-discovery claim difficult. The sources below document particular statements and distinguish them from the later history of the name.

Read the sources

  • Original publication Brianchon and Poncelet, Recherches sur la détermination d’une hyperbole équilatèreAnnales 11 (1820–1821), Théorème IX, printed pp. 215–216 (PDF pp. 12–13). The issue date is printed at the foot of p. 205.

Al-Muʾtaman · Eleventh century; Ceva · 1678

Ceva’s theorem before Ceva

The theorem named after Giovanni Ceva has an earlier surviving account in the Book of Perfection of al-Muʾtaman ibn Hūd, ruler of Zaragoza. Jan Hogendijk identifies the concurrency criterion, including both directions, as Proposition 312.18 in the surviving manuscript tradition. Whether al-Muʾtaman devised it or drew on an unidentified source is unresolved.

Ceva published the ratio relation in De lineis rectis in 1678. His book connects geometry with balancing weights; it also prints an area argument credited to Pietro Paolo Caravaggio the younger. That makes a useful comparison with our episode’s areas. These records do not establish a route of transmission from al-Muʾtaman to Ceva, but they show why a theorem’s name need not identify its earliest surviving appearance.
