The theorem

Cyclic quadrilaterals

A convex quadrilateral ABCD is cyclic if and only if ∠ABC + ∠ADC = 180°. The other pair of opposite angles is then supplementary as well.

Before the proof

The first three vertices determine a circle. What condition forces the fourth vertex D onto it?

Four distinct vertices in boundary order

The theorem concerns a convex quadrilateral. Here D moves on a ray from B that crosses the interior of AC, and stays beyond that crossing. Drag the purple circle point D′ along the arc CA that avoids B to change the ray. These constraints preserve the order A, B, C, D and exclude a collapsed or crossed quadrilateral.

The converse does not assume its conclusion

For the converse, start with a convex quadrilateral whose opposite angles are supplementary and draw only the circle through A, B, C. Its second intersection with ray BD is named D′. The two hypothetical positions D inside and D outside the circle lead to strict angle inequalities. Only after ruling them out do we identify D with D′.

The second intersection exists

The diagonals of a convex quadrilateral cross at an interior point X of AC. Since X is strictly inside the disk through A and C, the ray from B through X enters the disk and leaves it at a unique second circle point D′ beyond X. The interior of a chord lies inside the disk: dropping a perpendicular from the center to the chord and applying Pythagoras proves this directly.

Converse diagrams are hypothetical cases

The forward proof holds D on the circle. The converse shows an inside-circle test position and an outside-circle test position; their measured opposite angles do not add to 180°, which is the contradiction being proved. The given supplementary-angle condition is never inferred from a rounded measurement. Labels joined by ≈ indicate drawing proximity only.

Read the complete proof

This written argument is also available without the interactive diagram. The diagram illustrates the proof; measurements do not establish the result.

  1. Start with a cyclic quadrilateral

    Suppose A, B, C, D lie on a circle in that boundary order, forming a convex quadrilateral. Write α = ∠ABC and δ = ∠ADC.

    • Four distinct vertices in cyclic order

  2. Identify the two intercepted arcs

    Angle ABC intercepts the arc AC through D, while angle ADC intercepts the other arc AC, through B. These two arcs together make the whole circle.

    • measure of arc ADC + measure of arc ABC = 360°

  3. Halve the full turn

    The inscribed-angle theorem makes α and δ half the measures of their respective arcs. Adding gives α + δ = 180°.

    • α + δ = ½ · 360° = 180°

  4. The other opposite angles

    Apply the same argument to chord BD. The angles at A and C are supplementary as well.

    • ∠DAB + ∠BCD = 180°

  5. Assume the angle condition instead

    For the converse, let ABCD be convex and assume ∠ABC + ∠ADC = 180°. Draw the circle through A, B, C. We must prove that D lies on it.

    • The circle is defined by A, B, C only.
    • Cyclicity of ABCD has not been assumed.

  6. Construct the circle point on the same ray

    Let X = AC ∩ BD. Convexity puts X inside both diagonals, and the interior of chord AC lies inside the disk. Thus ray BX leaves the circle at a second point D′ beyond X. The forward direction applied to ABCD′ gives ∠AD′C = 180° − ∠ABC = ∠ADC.

    • B, X, D and B, X, D′ have these orders.
    • ∠AD′C = ∠ADC under the given hypothesis.

  7. Test the nearer candidate

    Suppose D lies between X and D′. In triangle ADD′, angle ADB is an exterior angle, so it is larger than angle AD′B. In triangle CDD′, angle BDC is similarly larger than angle BD′C.

    • ∠ADB > ∠AD′B
    • ∠BDC > ∠BD′C

  8. Add the inequalities

    The ray DB lies inside angle ADC, and the ray D′B lies inside angle AD′C. Adding the inequalities therefore gives ∠ADC > ∠AD′C, contradicting the equality forced by the hypothesis.

    • ∠ADC > ∠AD′C contradicts ∠ADC = ∠AD′C.

  9. Test the farther candidate

    If D lies beyond D′, interchange D and D′ in the same two exterior-angle arguments. They give ∠ADC < ∠AD′C, again contradicting the required equality.

    • ∠ADC < ∠AD′C is also impossible.

  10. Identify the fourth vertex

    Both distinct positions on the ray are impossible, so D = D′. Therefore D lies on the circle through A, B, C, and ABCD is cyclic. This proves the converse.

    • ABCD cyclic ⇔ ∠ABC + ∠ADC = 180°
