These ten problems connect triangle centers through reflections, apply Ceva and symmedian techniques in new configurations, and derive Heron’s formula from the incircle and an excircle. Problems 1–3 adapt selected exercises from Venema; Problems 7–8 follow his step-by-step derivation of Heron’s formula. The other problems apply ideas from the proof episodes.

Throughout, ABCABC is noncollinear. Write a=BCa=BC, b=CAb=CA, c=ABc=AB, s=(a+b+c)/2s=(a+b+c)/2, and Δ=[ABC]\Delta=[ABC]. Write G,O,H,I,K,Na,GeG,O,H,I,K,N_a,G_e for the centroid, circumcenter, orthocenter, incenter, symmedian point, Nagel point, and Gergonne point. The symbol NaN_a denotes the Nagel point, not the nine-point center. All applications of Ceva concern strictly interior side points, with ordinary positive lengths and sines. In Problems 1–3, conjugation is defined only for interior points; Problem 3 assumes that the triangle is acute.

These are the facts you may assume.

  • The Basics, ordinary Ceva, and trigonometric Ceva, with both concurrence criteria restricted to interior cevians.
  • The constructions of the familiar centers, the squared-side criterion for a symmedian, and the incircle and excircle contact facts in the Gergonne and Nagel episodes. A statement that a problem asks you to establish still requires a proof.
  • In Problems 7–8, use the incircle and excircle constructions, equal tangents, area additivity, and similarity. Derive the requested area–radius identities and Heron’s formula; do not assume those conclusions from the Heron episode. Their derivations follow Venema’s Exercises 4.5.1–4.5.7.
  • In Problems 9–10, you may use Heron’s formula and all the area–radius identities proved in Problems 7–8. Here rr is the inradius and rA,rB,rCr_A,r_B,r_C are the exradii opposite the indicated vertices.
  • For reconstruction, three positive numbers are side lengths of a noncollinear triangle exactly when each is smaller than the sum of the other two. SSS gives uniqueness up to congruence once corresponding side lengths are fixed. Earlier results in this set may be used in later problems.

Book references. Gerard A. Venema, Exploring Advanced Euclidean Geometry with GeoGebra (2013). Each adapted problem gives its exercise numbers and printed page; the parenthetical PDF page is the one-based viewer page in the course copy. Our added steps and interior-point restrictions are stated explicitly. The source’s starred exercises are investigations, so an added proof requirement is an adaptation.

1. Side reflections connect the Gergonne and Nagel points

Adapted from Venema, Exercises 8.6.1 and 8.6.6, printed p. 74 (PDF p. 91). We restrict the construction to interior points and add the four-part scaffold below. Use ordinary Ceva and the Gergonne and Nagel episodes.

Let PP lie strictly inside ABCABC. The rays AP,BP,CPAP,BP,CP meet the opposite sides at D,E,FD,E,F, respectively. Reflect DD across the perpendicular bisector of BCBC, obtaining D′D'; define E′E' and F′F' by the corresponding reflections on CACA and ABAB.

(a) Explain why D,E,F,D′,E′,F′D,E,F,D',E',F' all lie strictly inside their respective sides. Express BD′/D′CBD'/D'C in terms of BD/DCBD/DC, and write the two cyclic identities.

(b) Use ordinary Ceva to prove that AD′,BE′,CF′AD',BE',CF' meet at a unique interior point QQ. This point is called the isotomic conjugate of PP with respect to ABCABC.

(c) Apply the same construction to QQ. Prove that it returns PP. Such an operation is called an involution: applying it twice gives the original point.

(d) Now take P=GeP=G_e, so D,E,FD,E,F are the incircle contact points. Let TA,TB,TCT_A,T_B,T_C be the contacts of the opposite excircles with BC,CA,ABBC,CA,AB. Use the contact lengths established in the episodes to prove D′=TAD'=T_A, E′=TBE'=T_B, and F′=TCF'=T_C. Conclude that the Gergonne and Nagel points are isotomic conjugates of one another.

Hints for Problem 1
  • (a) A ray from a vertex through an interior point crosses the opposite side once. The reflection used on BCBC exchanges its endpoints; compare the distance from BB to the reflected point with the distance from CC to the original point.
  • (b) Start with the Ceva product for the three cevians through PP. Replace its factors using part (a). After applying the converse, explain why two distinct interior cevians can have only one common point.
  • (c) Identify the opposite-side intersections of the three cevians through QQ. What does reflecting each of these side points a second time do?
  • (d) On BCBC, compare the ordered pairs of lengths (BD,DC)(BD,DC) and (BTA,TAC)(BT_A,T_AC). A point on a fixed segment is determined by its distance from one endpoint. Use part (c) for the reverse correspondence.

2. Angle reflections and the incenter

Adapted from Venema, Exercises 8.6.2 and 8.6.8 and the interior-point investigation in starred Exercise 8.6.7, printed p. 74 (PDF p. 91). The investigation is made into a proof problem here, and all points remain interior. Use trigonometric Ceva and the symmedian episode.

Let PP lie strictly inside ABCABC, and let D,E,FD,E,F be the opposite-side intersections of AP,BP,CPAP,BP,CP. Reflect the ray APAP across the internal angle bisector at AA; reflect BPBP and CPCP across the corresponding bisectors at BB and CC.

(a) Prove that each reflected ray is still strictly inside its vertex angle and meets the opposite side at a unique interior point. Call the resulting points D′,E′,F′D',E',F'. Identify the two angles at AA that reflection exchanges.

(b) Use trigonometric Ceva to prove that AD′,BE′,CF′AD',BE',CF' meet at a unique interior point QQ. This point is called the isogonal conjugate of PP with respect to ABCABC. Explain why all sine ratios used in the argument are positive and defined.

(c) Prove that isogonal conjugation is an involution on the interior of the triangle. Identify the isogonal conjugate of the centroid GG, and use the involution to identify the isogonal conjugate of KK.

(d) Prove that Q=PQ=P if and only if P=IP=I. Thus the incenter is the unique interior point fixed by isogonal conjugation. Your argument must also cover isosceles and equilateral triangles.

Hints for Problem 2
  • (a) Reflection in the internal bisector interchanges the two boundary rays of the angle. If an interior ray cuts the angle into two positive parts, describe the parts cut out by its reflection.
  • (b) Write the sine ratio at AA before and after reflection, and then repeat at the other vertices. Multiply before applying trigonometric Ceva. Use the interior positions of the side points when discussing the point of concurrence.
  • (c) Reflect each of the three rays a second time. For the center identification, recall which cevians pass through GG and how a symmedian is defined.
  • (d) If Q=PQ=P, the reflected ray from each vertex must be the original ray. Compare the two positive angles it cuts out. For the converse, start with the rays from the vertices through II.

3. The circumcenter and orthocenter are isogonal conjugates

Adapted from Venema, Exercise 8.6.9, printed p. 75 (PDF p. 92). The acute-triangle restriction and the intermediate steps below are added here. Use the circumcenter and orthocenter episodes and Problem 2.

Assume that ABCABC is strictly acute, meaning that all three vertex angles are less than 90∘90^\circ. Let OO and HH be its circumcenter and orthocenter. In this problem you may use the following circle-angle fact from Venema, §0.10, printed p. 10 (PDF p. 27): if distinct points X,Y,ZX,Y,Z lie on a circle with center OO and ∠XZY<90∘\angle XZY<90^\circ, then the ordinary angle ∠XOY\angle XOY is twice ∠XZY\angle XZY. No directed angles are needed.

(a) Let DD be the altitude foot from AA on BCBC. Explain why all three altitude feet are interior to their sides and why HH lies inside ABCABC. Show that

∠BAH=90∘−∠B,\angle BAH=90^\circ-\angle B,∠HAC=90∘−∠C.\angle HAC=90^\circ-\angle C.

(b) Apply the supplied circle-angle fact to ∠AOB\angle AOB and ∠AOC\angle AOC. Use the two isosceles radius triangles to prove

∠BAO=90∘−∠C,\angle BAO=90^\circ-\angle C,∠OAC=90∘−∠B.\angle OAC=90^\circ-\angle B.

Explain why these equalities put the ray AOAO strictly inside ∠BAC\angle BAC. Repeat the position argument at another vertex to justify that OO lies inside ABCABC.

(c) Compare parts (a) and (b). Prove that reflection across the internal bisector at AA exchanges the rays AOAO and AHAH. Repeat at BB and CC, and conclude that OO and HH are isogonal conjugates of one another.

(d) Prove that O=HO=H if and only if ABCABC is equilateral. In the forward direction, compare an altitude through a vertex with the perpendicular bisector of the opposite side; in the reverse direction, explain why these lines coincide in an equilateral triangle.

Hints for Problem 3
  • (a) A perpendicular foot outside the opposite side would force one of the two angles at that side's endpoints to be nonacute. Two cevians joining vertices to interior points of their opposite sides meet inside the triangle. Then use the angle sums in ABDABD and ACDACD.
  • (b) The base angles of an isosceles triangle are half the difference between 180∘180^\circ and its vertex angle. Check that the two angles you obtain at AA are positive and add to ∠A\angle A. For an angle less than 180∘180^\circ, this equality places the intervening ray inside the angle; repeating at BB determines the required side of each sideline.
  • (c) Record the two angles on each side of AOAO, then do the same for AHAH. Compare their order, rather than merely their sum. Problem 2 turns the three reflected rays into a statement about conjugate points.
  • (d) If O=HO=H, both the altitude from AA and the perpendicular bisector of BCBC pass through this point and are perpendicular to BCBC. Use uniqueness of that perpendicular and the perpendicular-bisector property. Repeat at one other vertex.

4. When angle ratios give the wrong prediction

An application of trigonometric Ceva.

Let ABCABC be a right isosceles triangle with ∠A=90∘\angle A=90^\circ and AB=AC=1AB=AC=1. Choose interior side points D∈BCD\in BC, E∈CAE\in CA, and F∈ABF\in AB so that

∠BAD=60∘,∠DAC=30∘,\angle BAD=60^\circ,\quad \angle DAC=30^\circ,∠CBE=15∘,∠EBA=30∘,\angle CBE=15^\circ,\quad \angle EBA=30^\circ,

and CFCF is the internal angle bisector at CC. You may use sin⁡30∘=1/2\sin30^\circ=1/2, sin⁡60∘=3/2\sin60^\circ=\sqrt3/2, and sin⁡15∘=(6−2)/4\sin15^\circ=(\sqrt6-\sqrt2)/4.

(a) Find the two angles at CC cut out by CFCF, and compute the product

∠BAD∠DAC∠CBE∠EBA∠ACF∠FCB,\frac{\angle BAD}{\angle DAC} \frac{\angle CBE}{\angle EBA} \frac{\angle ACF}{\angle FCB},

where all six angles are measured in degrees. What would this product suggest if one mistakenly replaced the sine ratios in trigonometric Ceva by ratios of angles?

(b) Compute the correct product of sine ratios exactly. Prove that AD,BE,CFAD,BE,CF are not concurrent; a decimal approximation alone is not a proof.

(c) Keep DD and EE fixed. Find BD/DCBD/DC and CE/EACE/EA, and determine the ratio AF∗/F∗BAF_*/F_*B required for AD,BE,CF∗AD,BE,CF_* to be concurrent.

(d) Prove that there is exactly one such point F∗F_* in the interior of ABAB. Find AF∗/ABAF_*/AB, and decide whether F∗F_* lies between AA and FF or between FF and BB.

Hints for Problem 4
  • (a) First find the two acute angles of the right isosceles triangle. The bisector divides the angle at CC into equal parts. Keep the ordinary angle product separate from the sine product.
  • (b) Substitute the standard values of sin⁡30∘\sin30^\circ and sin⁡60∘\sin60^\circ, together with the supplied value of sin⁡15∘\sin15^\circ. After simplifying, you can test equality with 11 by squaring an equality between positive quantities.
  • (c) Use the side–sine identity from Episode 11 at AA and then at BB; the side-length factor at BB is not 11. Once two side ratios are known, ordinary Ceva determines the third.
  • (d) A positive ratio AF∗/F∗B=kAF_*/F_*B=k, together with AF∗+F∗B=ABAF_*+F_*B=AB, determines one point of the segment. Find the corresponding ratio for the original angle-bisector foot FF, and compare the two positions using exact inequalities between positive numbers.

5. The symmedian point of a right triangle

A geometric application of the symmedian episode.

Suppose ∠A=90∘\angle A=90^\circ. Let DD be the foot of the perpendicular from AA to BCBC, and let LL be the midpoint of ADAD.

(a) Explain why DD lies in the interior of BCBC. Use similar triangles to prove

BD=AB2BC,BD=\frac{AB^2}{BC},DC=AC2BC.DC=\frac{AC^2}{BC}.

(b) Prove that ADAD is the symmedian from AA. Then use area comparisons to show

[LBC]=[ABC]2,[LBC]=\frac{[ABC]}2,[LAB]=[ABD]2.[LAB]=\frac{[ABD]}2.

(c) Let the ray BLBL meet ACAC at EE. Compare areas to find CE/EACE/EA, and prove that BLBL is the symmedian from BB. Deduce that K=LK=L. If AB=3AB=3 and AC=4AC=4, find AKAK exactly.

(d) Now allow ABCABC to be any noncollinear triangle whose altitude foot DD lies strictly inside BCBC. Prove that ADAD is a symmedian if and only if either AB=ACAB=AC or ∠A=90∘\angle A=90^\circ.

Hints for Problem 5
  • (a) The angles at BB and CC are acute. In the two smaller right triangles, identify which acute angle also occurs in ABCABC, and keep corresponding sides in the same order.
  • (b) Use the squared-side criterion for a symmedian. For the area equalities, one comparison uses base BCBC and the other uses bases AL,ADAL,AD on the same line.
  • (c) Compare [LBC][LBC] with [EBC][EBC] using bases on BEBE, and make the same comparison with [LAB][LAB] and [EAB][EAB]. The common factor cancels. Then compare the latter two triangles using bases on ACAC. For the numerical value, compute the same total area using the legs and using the altitude to the hypotenuse.
  • (d) Write x=BDx=BD, y=DCy=DC, and h=ADh=AD. Express AB2AB^2 and AC2AC^2 by Pythagoras, substitute into the symmedian criterion, and factor the resulting equality. Interpret each factor geometrically; use the converse of Pythagoras when needed.

6. The cevian that balances the perimeter

An application of the Nagel point.

Let TAT_A be the contact of the AA-excircle with BCBC. You may use BTA=s−cBT_A=s-c and TAC=s−bT_AC=s-b. A boundary path from AA to a point of BCBC follows the sides through either BB or CC; its length does not include the cevian joining its endpoints.

(a) Show that the two boundary paths A ⁣→B ⁣→TAA\!\to B\!\to T_A and A ⁣→C ⁣→TAA\!\to C\!\to T_A have the same length, and identify that length.

(b) Let DD be any point strictly inside BCBC. Prove that the triangles ABDABD and ACDACD have equal perimeters if and only if D=TAD=T_A. Explain why this gives a unique perimeter-balancing cevian from AA.

(c) Suppose (a,b,c)=(16,14,10)(a,b,c)=(16,14,10). Find BTABT_A, TACT_AC, and the common boundary-path length. Express the common perimeter of the two triangles in terms of ATAAT_A, and find [ABTA]:[ACTA][ABT_A]:[ACT_A]. Does balancing the perimeters also balance the areas in this example?

(d) At each of A,B,CA,B,C, draw the unique cevian that splits ABCABC into two triangles of equal perimeter. Use the Nagel episode to prove that these three cevians have a unique common point. State this as a new geometric characterization of NaN_a, without repeating a Ceva proof.

Hints for Problem 6
  • (a) The path through BB has length c+BTAc+BT_A; use the corresponding expression through CC.
  • (b) Both small-triangle perimeters contain ADAD, so cancel it. If BD=tBD=t, substitute DC=a−tDC=a-t; a prescribed distance from BB determines at most one point of BCBC.
  • (c) Keep the boundary path separate from the closed perimeter: the latter also includes ATAAT_A. For the area ratio, use the common height from AA to BCBC.
  • (d) Apply part (b) at each vertex. Identify the three resulting side points before invoking the known Nagel concurrence theorem.

7. The first half of Heron's proof

Source: Venema, §4.5, Exercises 4.5.1–4.5.4, printed p. 45 (PDF p. 62); contact labels follow Figure 4.4 on printed p. 44 (PDF p. 61). The exercises are grouped into four parts below.

Let ABCABC be a noncollinear triangle with side lengths a=BCa=BC, b=CAb=CA, c=ABc=AB, semiperimeter s=(a+b+c)/2s=(a+b+c)/2, and area Δ\Delta. Its incircle has center II, radius rr, and contact points X∈BCX\in BC, Y∈CAY\in CA, and Z∈ABZ\in AB. The AA-excircle has center EE, radius rAr_A, and contact points TT on BCBC, UU on the extension of ACAC beyond CC, and VV on the extension of ABAB beyond BB.

You may use the geometric facts established in the incenter and Nagel episodes: II is interior and AI,BI,CIAI,BI,CI are the internal angle bisectors; the incircle contacts lie strictly inside their sides; the excircle contacts satisfy B ⁣− ⁣T ⁣− ⁣CB\! -\! T\! -\! C, A ⁣− ⁣C ⁣− ⁣UA\! -\! C\! -\! U, and A ⁣− ⁣B ⁣− ⁣VA\! -\! B\! -\! V; the ray AEAE follows the same internal angle bisector as AIAI; and BEBE bisects the exterior angle between BCBC and BVBV. Both radii are positive, and each radius to a contact point is perpendicular to its sideline. Do not use Heron's formula in Problems 7–8: these problems will prove it.

(a) Divide ABCABC into the three triangles with vertex II, and prove

Δ=sr.\Delta=sr.

(b) Set

z=AZ,z=AZ,x=BX,x=BX,y=CY,y=CY,u=CU,u=CU,v=BV.v=BV.

Use equal tangent lengths and the stated contact order to prove

a=x+y,b=y+z,c=x+z,s=x+y+z,a=x+y,\quad b=y+z,\quad c=x+z,\quad s=x+y+z,

and hence x=s−bx=s-b, y=s−cy=s-c, and z=s−az=s-a. Next establish

u+v=x+y,u+v=x+y,x+z+v=y+z+u,x+z+v=y+z+u,

and deduce u=xu=x and v=yv=y. Explain why all five tangent lengths are positive.

(c) Show that the full tangent segment from AA to the excircle satisfies AV=sAV=s.

(d) Prove △AZI∼△AVE\triangle AZI\sim\triangle AVE, stating the vertex correspondence, and deduce

rAs=rs−a.\frac{r_A}{s}=\frac{r}{s-a}.
Hints for Problem 7
  • (a) Use ABAB, BCBC, and CACA as bases. What is the corresponding height from II in each triangle?
  • (b) Pair the tangents from each of A,B,CA,B,C. Along BCBC, add BT+TCBT+TC; along the two rays from AA, compare AB+BVAB+BV with AC+CUAC+CU. Add and subtract the resulting equations for u,vu,v.
  • (c) Write AV=AB+BVAV=AB+BV, and use the expressions from part (b).
  • (d) Locate the two right angles and the common half-angle at AA. Match the side opposite that half-angle with the adjacent tangent segment in each triangle.

8. Finish the proof of Heron's formula

Source: Venema, §4.5, Exercises 4.5.5–4.5.7, printed p. 45 (PDF p. 62), form parts (a)–(c). Part (d) is an added consequence of the identities proved in Problems 7–8.

Keep all the notation and geometric facts from Problem 7, and put β=∠ABC\beta=\angle ABC. You may use every identity proved in Problem 7, but not Heron's formula before you derive it in part (c).

(a) Use the internal bisector BIBI and exterior bisector BEBE to prove

∠ZBI=β2,\angle ZBI=\frac{\beta}{2},∠EBV=180∘−β2.\angle EBV=\frac{180^\circ-\beta}{2}.

Explain why BI⊥BEBI\perp BE. Then use the angle sum in the right triangle VEBVEB to prove △ZBI∼△VEB\triangle ZBI\sim\triangle VEB, with correspondence Z↔VZ\leftrightarrow V, B↔EB\leftrightarrow E, I↔BI\leftrightarrow B.

(b) Read the corresponding sides in this similarity to prove

s−br=rAs−c,\frac{s-b}{r}=\frac{r_A}{s-c},and hence\text{and hence}rrA=(s−b)(s−c).rr_A=(s-b)(s-c).

(c) Combine part (b) with the radius relation from Problem 7(d) to obtain

r2=(s−a)(s−b)(s−c)s.r^2=\frac{(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}{s}.

Use Problem 7(a) to deduce Heron's formula

Δ=s(s−a)(s−b)(s−c).\Delta=\sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}.

Explain why every division is legitimate and why the positive square root is required.

(d) Let rB,rCr_B,r_C denote the radii of the excircles opposite B,CB,C. Derive, without any further construction,

Δ=sr=(s−a)rA=(s−b)rB=(s−c)rC.\Delta=sr=(s-a)r_A=(s-b)r_B=(s-c)r_C.

Explain why the proofs of the last two equalities follow by cyclically relabeling the triangle.

Hints for Problem 8
  • (a) The angles ABCABC and CBVCBV are supplementary. Their half-angles add to 90∘90^\circ. In VEBVEB, the angles at BB and EE are complementary.
  • (b) In the stated correspondence, BZBZ matches EVEV, and ZIZI matches VBVB. Substitute the lengths established in Problem 7.
  • (c) First express rAr_A in terms of r,s,s−ar,s,s-a. Substitute into part (b), then square the already-proved identity Δ=sr\Delta=sr.
  • (d) Cross-multiply Problem 7(d) and compare with Δ=sr\Delta=sr. Replace (A,B,C)(A,B,C) by (B,C,A)(B,C,A), and then by (C,A,B)(C,A,B).

9. The largest area with a fixed base and perimeter

An application of Heron’s formula.

Fix numbers a>0a>0 and s>as>a. Consider all triangles whose base BCBC has length aa and whose perimeter is 2s2s; the other two sides may vary.

(a) Put d=b−cd=b-c. Express bb and cc in terms of a,s,da,s,d, and prove that the admissible values of dd are exactly −a<d<a-a<d<a. Check both positivity and all three strict triangle inequalities in the converse direction.

(b) Show that

(s−b)+(s−c)=a,(s-b)+(s-c)=a,(s−b)−(s−c)=−d.(s-b)-(s-c)=-d.

Use a difference of squares and Heron’s formula to deduce

Δ2=s(s−a)4(a2−d2).\Delta^2=\frac{s(s-a)}4\bigl(a^2-d^2\bigr).

(c) Determine the largest possible area and characterize the triangles that attain it. Explain why the maximizing side lengths really occur in a noncollinear triangle and determine that triangle up to congruence.

(d) Suppose a positive number qq is prescribed as the area. Determine exactly when such a triangle exists, and recover b,cb,c, up to interchange, from a,s,qa,s,q. Explain what happens when qq equals the maximum and why q=0q=0 is excluded.

Hints for Problem 9
  • (a) Solve the two equations b+c=2s−ab+c=2s-a and b−c=db-c=d. For a triangle, compare b−cb-c and c−bc-b with aa; conversely, use s>as>a together with the two bounds on dd.
  • (b) For any numbers u,vu,v, the identity 4uv=(u+v)2−(u−v)24uv=(u+v)^2-(u-v)^2 expresses their product using their sum and difference. Apply it to u=s−bu=s-b, v=s−cv=s-c.
  • (c) The factor s(s−a)/4s(s-a)/4 is fixed and positive. Decide which admissible value of dd makes the remaining factor largest, then use part (a) to check that equality is attainable.
  • (d) Substitute Δ=q\Delta=q in part (b) and solve for d2d^2. A real square root is necessary, but also check the strict bound from part (a); the two possible signs of dd exchange bb and cc.

10. Reconstruct a triangle from its circle radii

An inverse problem using Heron’s formula and the tangent lengths from the Gergonne and Nagel episodes.

Four positive numbers are proposed as the inradius rr and the labeled exradii rA,rB,rCr_A,r_B,r_C of a triangle. We will determine exactly when this proposal is possible and whether it determines the triangle.

(a) For any triangle, use the area-radius identities proved in Problems 7–8 to prove

1r=1rA+1rB+1rC.\frac1r=\frac1{r_A}+\frac1{r_B}+\frac1{r_C}.

Apply this necessary test to the proposed quadruples

(r,rA,rB,rC)=(1,2,3,6)and(1,2,3,5).(r,r_A,r_B,r_C)=(1,2,3,6) \quad\text{and}\quad (1,2,3,5).

Which is ruled out, and which remains possible? Explain why passing a necessary test alone has not yet proved existence.

(b) Now suppose the four positive numbers satisfy the identity in part (a). The letters x,y,zx,y,z below are redefined for this problem. Define

Q=r rArBrC,Q=\sqrt{r\,r_A r_B r_C},x=QrA,y=QrB,z=QrC,x=\frac Q{r_A},\quad y=\frac Q{r_B},\quad z=\frac Q{r_C},

and propose side lengths

a=y+z,a=y+z,b=z+x,b=z+x,c=x+y.c=x+y.

Prove that these lengths form a noncollinear triangle. If SS is its semiperimeter, show that S=x+y+z=Q/rS=x+y+z=Q/r and identify S−a,S−b,S−cS-a,S-b,S-c. Explain how x,y,zx,y,z describe its incircle tangent lengths from the vertices.

(c) Use Heron’s formula to prove that the constructed triangle has area QQ. Then use the area-radius identities to show that its inradius and three labeled exradii are exactly the four proposed numbers. Conclude that the condition in part (a) is sufficient as well as necessary.

(d) Prove that any triangle with the proposed radii must have the side lengths constructed in part (b), so the triangle is unique up to congruence with corresponding vertex labels. Reconstruct the candidate from part (a) that passes the test: find its area and three sides, and identify the resulting kind of triangle.

Hints for Problem 10
  • (a) Divide each of s,s−a,s−b,s−cs,s-a,s-b,s-c by the area using the identities from Problems 7–8. Add the three expressions involving exradii and use a+b+c=2sa+b+c=2s; keep the candidate tests as exact fractions.
