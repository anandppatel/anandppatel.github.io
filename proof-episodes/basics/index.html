The Basics

Facts we may use without reproving them.

Use these statements in the proof episodes, checking the hypotheses each time. Cite the label and, when needed, the named rule: T1 (SAS) or O2 (equal tangents). A problem may give a narrower list of allowed tools.

We will add tools as we need them. Each label has a permanent link, so earlier episodes can keep citing it.

Conventions and how to read the diagrams

We work in the Euclidean plane. A triangle has three noncollinear vertices, and a circle has positive radius. AB denotes the length of the segment joining A and B. For distinct points A and B, the line through them extends in both directions. In ∠ABC, B is the vertex. Angles are non-reflex and measured in degrees.

A definition does not itself establish existence or uniqueness. Use a converse when it is explicitly stated here. Diagrams illustrate the statements: an apparent equality, right angle, parallelism, or concurrence is not an additional hypothesis.

The illustrations show the hypotheses and conclusions. They are not proofs.

Constructions

C1 Perpendiculars and distance

Through any point P there is exactly one line perpendicular to a given line ℓ. If P is not on ℓ, its intersection H with ℓ is the foot of the perpendicular, and PH is the distance from P to ℓ. A point on ℓ has distance zero from ℓ.

An altitude is the perpendicular drawn from a vertex to the line containing the opposite side. Its foot may lie on the side, at an endpoint, or beyond an endpoint.

C2 Rigid motions and congruence

A rigid motion, or isometry, is a bijection of the plane that preserves distances. We may apply translations, rotations, reflections, and their compositions. Every point of a figure moves by the same motion.

Rigid motions take lines to lines, segments to segments, and circles to circles. They preserve angles, betweenness, parallelism, perpendicularity, and area. A reflection may reverse a figure's orientation.

Two figures are congruent if a rigid motion carries one onto the other. Corresponding lengths and angles are equal.

C3 Similitudes and scale

A similitude of scale factor k > 0 is a bijection S of the plane satisfying S(A)S(B) = k·AB for every pair A, B.

A dilation with center O and factor k > 0 fixes O and sends P ≠ O to the point P′ on ray OP with OP′ = k·OP. We may apply dilations and compose them with rigid motions.

Similitudes preserve angles, betweenness, parallelism, and perpendicularity. Lengths are multiplied by k, and polygon areas by k². Two figures are similar if a similitude carries one onto the other. A scale factor of 1 gives a rigid motion.

C4 Area

  • Rectangle: area = bh for side lengths b and h.
  • Triangle: area = ½bh, where h is the perpendicular distance from the opposite vertex to the line containing the base of length b. The foot of the altitude may lie outside the side.
  • Additivity: if a polygon is partitioned into finitely many polygonal pieces with disjoint interiors, its area is the sum of their areas. The polygonal boundaries contribute no area.
  • Comparison: triangles with equal bases and equal heights have equal areas. With a common height, their areas have the same ratio as their base lengths.

Rigid motions preserve area.

C5 Straight angles and vertical angles: “X”

A right angle measures 90°; a straight angle measures 180°. Adjacent angles that form a straight angle add to 180°.

When two distinct lines intersect, opposite (vertical) angles are equal. Adjacent angles need not be equal.

Triangle Stuff

T1 Congruence and isosceles triangles

△ABC ≅ △DEF specifies A ↔ D, B ↔ E, C ↔ F. Congruent triangles have equal corresponding side lengths and angle measures. Each test below establishes congruence.

  • SAS: two corresponding sides and their included angle are equal.
  • SSS: all three corresponding sides are equal.
  • ASA: two corresponding angles and their included side are equal.
  • HL: both triangles are right triangles, their hypotenuses are equal, and one pair of corresponding legs is equal. The hypotenuse lies opposite the right angle.
  • Two angles and a side: two corresponding angles and any one corresponding side are equal.

Two sides and a non-included angle (SSA) do not in general establish congruence.

Isosceles triangle theorem and converse. An isosceles triangle has at least two equal sides. In △ABC,

AB = AC ⇔ ∠ABC = ∠ACB.

Equal sides give equal base angles; equal base angles give equal sides.

T2 Angle sum and exterior angles

The interior angles of a triangle add to 180°:

∠BAC + ∠ABC + ∠ACB = 180°.

If BC is extended through C to D, with B, C, D in that order, then

∠ACD = ∠BAC + ∠ABC.

An exterior angle is greater than either of its two remote interior angles.

T3 Sides and opposite angles

Within one triangle, a greater side lies opposite a greater angle, and conversely. Equal sides lie opposite equal angles, and conversely:

AB > AC ⇔ ∠ACB > ∠ABC,

AB = AC ⇔ ∠ACB = ∠ABC.

These comparisons concern sides and angles of the same triangle.

T4 Triangle inequality

Each side of a triangle is shorter than the sum of the other two. In particular,

AB < AC + CB, and |AB − AC| < BC < AB + AC.

For any three points, AB ≤ AC + CB. Equality holds exactly when C lies on the closed segment from A to B, including either endpoint. A nondegenerate triangle gives strict inequality.

T5 Triangle similarity: AA / AAA

Two equal pairs of corresponding angles establish triangle similarity. This is AA, equivalently AAA.

For △ABC ∼ △DEF, the correspondence is A ↔ D, B ↔ E, C ↔ F, and

DE/AB = EF/BC = FD/CA = k > 0.

The area of △DEF is k² times the area of △ABC. Equal corresponding angles do not by themselves give congruence: the scale factor need not be 1.

T6 Pythagoras and its converse

In a right triangle with leg lengths a, b and hypotenuse length c,

a² + b² = c².

Conversely, if a triangle has side lengths a, b, c satisfying this equation, its angle opposite c is a right angle.

Parallel Stuff

P1 Alternate interior angles: “Z”

Two distinct lines are parallel if they do not meet. A transversal meets two distinct lines at different points. At either intersection, interior angles have a side along the transversal pointing toward the other intersection. An alternate interior pair contains one such angle at each intersection, on opposite sides of the transversal.

If the lines are parallel, alternate interior angles are equal. Conversely, equality of a pair of alternate interior angles establishes parallelism.

The “Z” identifies the angle pair; its appearance alone does not establish parallelism.

P2 Other angles across parallel lines

For two parallel lines cut by a transversal:

  • Corresponding angles, in matching positions at the two intersections, are equal.
  • Same-side interior angles add to 180°.

Both converses are allowed: an equal corresponding pair, or a same-side interior pair with sum 180°, establishes parallelism.

Circle Stuff

O1 Tangents and radii

A tangent is a line that meets a circle in exactly one point, its point of tangency.

For a circle with center O, a line tangent at T is perpendicular to OT. Conversely, if T lies on the circle, the line through T perpendicular to OT is tangent there.

There is exactly one tangent at each point of a circle. The radius and tangent meet at that point of tangency.

O2 Equal tangents: “party hats”

From a point P strictly outside a circle with center O, there are exactly two tangent lines. If they touch the circle at A and B, then

PA = PB.

Both segments start at the same exterior point and end at points of tangency on the same circle. The radii OA and OB are perpendicular to PA and PB, respectively.

For a circle of center O and radius r, the number of tangent lines through P is:

  • Two if OP > r.
  • One if OP = r.
  • None if OP < r.
