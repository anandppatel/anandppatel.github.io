The theorem

Brahmagupta’s formula

For a convex cyclic quadrilateral with consecutive side lengths a, b, c, d and semiperimeter s = (a + b + c + d)/2, the area K is √((s − a)(s − b)(s − c)(s − d)).

Before the proof

Heron’s formula uses three side lengths. What changes when a fourth side closes on the same circle?

The hypotheses matter

A, B, C, D are four distinct vertices in boundary order on one circle, so ABCD is convex. The diagonal AC partitions its interior into triangles ABC and ADC. The result is not an area formula for an arbitrary quadrilateral with the same four side lengths.

A local cosine-law lemma

The proof does not assume the cosine law as an earlier theorem. Let x be the signed projection of BA along BC and h the perpendicular height from A. Then a² = x² + h² and AC² = (b − x)² + h²; subtracting gives AC² = a² + b² − 2bx. Since x = a cos θ, this proves the needed cosine law, including obtuse angles.

Sine and cosine conventions

For an angle θ between 0° and 180°, the sine is the nonnegative perpendicular component of its unit direction and the cosine is its signed parallel component. Pythagoras gives sin² θ + cos² θ = 1. Reflecting a direction gives sin(180° − θ) = sin θ and cos(180° − θ) = −cos θ. Thus the projection remains signed when a foot lies on an extension.

Comparison with Heron

Heron’s formula appears as the limiting expression when one side tends to zero. A quadrilateral with a zero side is outside the distinct-vertex hypotheses. The argument here uses neither Heron nor Ptolemy as an input.

Two independent measurements

The displayed polygon area is computed from oriented triangle areas, separately from the expression involving the four side lengths. Rounded agreement illustrates the formula; the proof establishes it.

Read the complete proof

This written argument is also available without the interactive diagram. The diagram illustrates the proof; measurements do not establish the result.

  1. Name the sides

    Let ABCD be convex and cyclic, with consecutive side lengths a = AB, b = BC, c = CD, d = DA, semiperimeter s, and area K.

    • 2s = a + b + c + d

  2. Use cyclicity

    Write θ = angle ABC. Opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral are supplementary, so angle ADC = 180° − θ. The diagonal AC partitions the quadrilateral into two triangles.

    • ∠ADC = 180° − θ
    • K = [ABC] + [ADC]

  3. Express each triangle area

    The perpendicular height from A to line BC is a sin θ, so [ABC] = ab sin θ/2. The same height argument at D gives [ADC] = cd sin(180° − θ)/2.

    • 2[ABC] = ab sin θ
    • 2[ADC] = cd sin(180° − θ)

  4. Add the areas

    Supplementary angles have the same sine. Adding the two triangle areas therefore gives one equation for K.

    • 2K = (ab + cd) sin θ

  5. Prove the needed cosine law

    Let F be the perpendicular projection of A onto line BC. Measure x = BF with sign in the direction from B to C, and let h = AF. Thus x = a cos θ, including when θ is obtuse.

    • x = a cos θ
    • h = a sin θ

  6. Two applications of Pythagoras

    Pythagoras in the two perpendicular decompositions gives a² = x² + h² and AC² = (b − x)² + h². These squared-length identities also hold when F equals B or C.

    • a² = x² + h²
    • AC² = (b − x)² + h²

  7. Subtract to remove the height

    Subtract the first equality from the second and use x = a cos θ. This proves the cosine law required here rather than assuming it.

    • AC² = a² + b² − 2ab cos θ

  8. Measure the diagonal from D

    Apply the same cosine-law lemma to triangle ADC. Since cos(180° − θ) = −cos θ, the sign of its cosine term is reversed.

    • AC² = c² + d² + 2cd cos θ

  9. Equate the diagonal formulas

    Equating the two expressions for AC² and collecting the cosine terms gives a second equation.

    • 2(ab + cd) cos θ = a² + b² − c² − d²

  10. Eliminate the angle

    Square the area equation, use sin² θ = 1 − cos² θ, and substitute the last equality.

    • 16K² = 4(ab + cd)² − (a² + b² − c² − d²)²

  11. Factor the difference of squares

    Factor first as a difference of squares, then regroup the two factors into differences of squared sums and differences.

    • 16K² = ((c + d)² − (a − b)²)((a + b)² − (c − d)²)

  12. Recognize the semiperimeter factors

    Factoring once more gives the four factors −a + b + c + d, a − b + c + d, a + b − c + d, and a + b + c − d. They equal 2(s − a), 2(s − b), 2(s − c), and 2(s − d).

    • 16K² = 16(s − a)(s − b)(s − c)(s − d)

  13. Brahmagupta’s formula

    Every side is shorter than the other three combined, so the four semiperimeter factors are positive. Divide by 16 and take the positive square root, since K is an area.

    • K = √((s − a)(s − b)(s − c)(s − d))
